# Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012) — what the paper actually says

Read 2026-09-09 in the author copy at
`w4.stern.nyu.edu/facdir/lpederse/papers/TimeSeriesMomentum.pdf`
(Journal of Financial Economics 104 (2012) 228–250). Quotations below are verbatim; the
page/section markers are the paper's own. Everything in `../SKILL.md` marked
"✅ source-verified" is one of these.

## Section 2.4 — the ex-ante volatility estimate, eq. (1)

> Specifically, the ex ante annualized variance `σ²_t` for each instrument is calculated as
> follows:

```
sigma_t^2 = 261 * sum_{i=0..inf} (1 - d) * d^i * (r_{t-1-i} - rbar_t)^2        (1)
```

> where the scalar 261 scales the variance to be annual, the weights `(1−δ)δ^i` add up to
> one, and `r̄_t` is the exponentially weighted average return computed similarly. The
> parameter `δ` is chosen so that the center of mass of the weights is
> `Σ_{i=0}^∞ (1−δ)δ^i i = δ/(1−δ) = 60 days`. The volatility model is the same for all
> assets at all times. While all of the results in the paper are robust to more
> sophisticated volatility models, we chose this model due to its simplicity and lack of
> look-ahead bias in the volatility estimate. **To ensure no look-ahead bias contaminates
> our results, we use the volatility estimates at time `t−1` applied to time-`t` returns
> throughout the analysis.**

Two separate facts, and both matter:

1. **eq. (1) sums over `r_{t−1−i}`.** `σ_t` never contains `r_t`. The lag is inside the
   estimator, not bolted on afterwards.
2. **and they lag it again** at the point of use. In the daily indexing these are the same
   statement; the paper says it twice because it is the one thing that silently inflates
   every number downstream.

`δ = 60/61 = 0.983607`. pandas' `com` is exactly this centre of mass — `ewm(com=60)`
decays by `com/(1+com) = 60/61`, so `δ/(1−δ) = 60`.

## Section 3.1 — the predictive regression, eq. (2)

```
r^s_t / sigma^s_{t-1} = a + b_h * r^s_{t-h} / sigma^s_{t-h-1} + e^s_t          (2)
```

> Given the vast differences in volatilities (as shown in Table 1), we divide all returns
> by their volatility to put them on the same scale.

Both sides are scaled, and both use the **lagged** sigma.

## Section 3.2 — the (k, h) grid, no overlap

> For each trading strategy (k,h), we derive a single time series of monthly returns even
> if the holding period h is more than one month. Hence, we do not have overlapping
> observations. We derive this single time series of returns following the methodology
> used by Jegadeesh and Titman (1993): The return at time t represents the average return
> across all portfolios at that time, namely the return on the portfolio that was
> constructed last month, the month before that (and still held if the holding period h is
> greater than two), and so on for all currently "active" portfolios.

> Specifically, for each instrument, we compute the time-t return based on the sign of the
> past return from time `t−k−1` to `t−1`. We then compute the time-t return based on the
> sign of the past return from `t−k−2` to `t−2`, and so on […]

The signal window ends at `t−1` and the return earned is `t−1 → t`. **Contiguous. No gap.**

## Section 4.1 — TSMOM itself, eq. (5)

> Following the convention used in the cross-sectional momentum literature (and based on
> the results from Fig. 1 and Table 2), we focus on the properties of the 12-month time
> series momentum strategy with a 1-month holding period (e.g., `k=12` and `h=1`), which we
> refer to simply as TSMOM.

> We size each position (long or short) so that it has an ex ante annualized volatility of
> 40%. That is, the position size is chosen to be `40%/σ_{t−1}`, where `σ_{t−1}` is the
> estimate of the ex ante volatility of the contract as described above. **The choice of
> 40% is inconsequential**, but it makes it easier to intuitively compare our portfolios to
> others in the literature. The 40% annual volatility is chosen because it is similar to
> the risk of an average individual stock, and when we average the return across all
> securities (equal-weighted) to form the portfolio of securities which represent our
> TSMOM factor, it has an annualized volatility of 12% per year over the sample period
> 1985–2009 […]

```
r^{TSMOM,s}_{t,t+1} = sign(r^s_{t-12,t}) * (40% / sigma^s_t) * r^s_{t,t+1}     (5)
```

and the diversified factor, immediately below (unnumbered):

```
r^{TSMOM}_{t,t+1} = (1/S_t) * sum_{s=1..S_t} sign(r^s_{t-12,t}) * (40% / sigma^s_t)
                                            * r^s_{t,t+1}
```

⚠️ **The prose writes `40%/σ_{t−1}` and eq. (5) writes `40%/σ_t`.** They are the same
quantity, because eq. (1) defines `σ_t` from `r_{t−1}, r_{t−2}, …`. If you re-implement
from eq. (5) alone with a `σ` that includes the contemporaneous return, you have silently
built the look-ahead the paper spent a paragraph avoiding.

## Footnote 10, p. 240 — the only mention of a skipped month

> Asness, Moskowitz, and Pedersen (2010) exclude the most recent month when computing
> 12-month cross-sectional momentum. For consistency, we follow that convention here, but
> our results do not depend on whether the most recent month is excluded or not.

This footnote hangs off the **cross-sectional** (XSMOM) comparison in Section 5, not off
eq. (5). TSMOM's lookback `r_{t−12,t}` runs up to the rebalance date.

## What is NOT in the paper

- No claim that daily rebalancing works — the strategy is monthly (`k=12, h=1`).
- No Donchian channel, no moving-average crossover. Those are in this skill because they
  are what people actually mean by "trend following"; they are not MOP's.
- The transaction costs in Section 5.3 are a separate exercise from eq. (5), which is
  gross.
